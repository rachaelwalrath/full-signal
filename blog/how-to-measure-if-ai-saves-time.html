How Do You Know If AI Actually Saved You Any Time?

WorkflowsOct 19, 20265 min read
Rachael WalrathFounder & Principal Consultant, Full Signal · LinkedIn

You're paying for at least one AI tool. Ask yourself how many hours it gave you back last month, and the honest answer is probably a shrug.

Nobody measured the before, so there's nothing to hold the after against.

The surveys won't settle it for you either. In July, Bluevine, a business bank that sells to the owners it surveyed, published results from 942 small business owners: AI is saving almost half of them (48%) more than four hours a week, and 78% still don't trust it to handle basic tasks (Bluevine, July 2026). Both can be true at once, and neither tells you which half you're in.

You can find out in two weeks, with a notepad.

Before you start: pick three tasks

Pick three things you do every week that AI is supposed to help with. Good candidates are the ones you can name in one line:

  • Turning call notes into a follow-up email
  • Drafting a proposal from a past one
  • Writing the weekly client status update

Skip anything that happens once a quarter. You need enough repetitions in two weeks to see a pattern.

Weeks 1 and 2: log the minutes

Every time you do one of the three, write down the task, the minutes from start to sent, and whether you had to fix what the tool gave you. A notes app works. So does a sticky note by the keyboard.

Two rules keep the log honest:

  1. Start the clock when you open the task, not when you open the tool. The ten minutes spent finding last month's proposal count.
  2. Count the fix-up time. A draft that took two minutes to generate and fifteen to repair took seventeen.

If you already use AI on these tasks, run week 1 with it and week 2 without. If you don't yet, run week 1 the old way and week 2 with the tool. Either way you end up with a before and an after for the same tasks, done by the same person, in the same month.

Day 15: do the arithmetic

For each task, take the minutes before, subtract the minutes after, and multiply by how many times a week you do it. Add the three together. That's your number.

Illustrative arithmetic, not a client result: if a follow-up email drops from 25 minutes to 8 and you send six a week, that's about 100 minutes a week from one task.

Then decide task by task:

  • It saved time and the output was good. Keep it, and write down exactly how you use it so you do it the same way every time.
  • It saved time, but you kept fixing the output. The tool doesn't know enough about how you work. That's a setup problem, and usually a fixable one.
  • It saved no time. Stop using it for that task. If that was the subscription's only job, cancel it.

What the number is for

Your number is about your week, your clients and your way of working. It also shows you something a survey can't: where the time went. If the follow-up email saved 100 minutes and the proposal saved none, you know where your next hour of setup belongs.

And it's the number to bring to renewal season. A tool that can't show you an hour a week doesn't need to come into 2027 with you.
