Heads Up for Small Law Firms: Skipping the Check Is What Gets You Sanctioned

WorkflowsNov 9, 20265 min read
Rachael WalrathFounder & Principal Consultant, Full Signal · LinkedIn

In June, a federal judge in Mississippi canceled a trial because she could no longer trust the lawyers on either side. Both sides had filed briefs citing court decisions that do not exist. All four attorneys were fined, removed from the case, and reported to their state bars. (Above the Law, Mississippi Free Press, Lawyer Monthly)

If you run a small practice and anyone in it drafts with AI, read what happened to each of them. One of them could have been you on a busy Thursday.

What happened

A solo practitioner used an AI drafting platform. Her motion cited two cases that don't exist. She was fined $2,500 and barred for two years from appearing in that district. She told the court she didn't know AI could make up citations. A bankruptcy court in Louisiana had disciplined her for the same thing two months earlier.

A partner on the other side used a different AI research tool. It produced four fake cases across two filings. She was fined $3,500 and given the same two-year bar. Her firm had a written policy requiring lawyers to verify research. She didn't follow it.

Two local counsel signed off on the filings without checking the citations. They were fined $1,000 each and removed from the case.

The pattern

Two different tools. A solo and a partner at an established firm. One lawyer had no policy; the other had one and skipped it. In every version, nobody opened the cited cases before the brief was filed.

A policy is a document. A check is a step with a name next to it. Only the second one would have stopped this.

None of the rules are new. Signing a filing certifies the law in it; that is Rule 11. The ABA's Formal Opinion 512 (July 2024) says lawyers don't have to be experts in the AI they use, but they do have to understand it well enough and review its output before relying on it. (ABA, July 2024)

Why it lands harder on a small firm

At a large firm, a brief passes through more than one set of eyes before it's filed. At a two or three lawyer practice, the person who drafted it is often the person who files it. If the check isn't written into the workflow, there's no second reader to catch what the first one missed.

The cost is uneven too. A two-year bar from a federal district is an inconvenience for a large firm that can staff the case with someone else. For a solo, it's a slice of the practice gone.

The check, written down

Put this between "draft done" and "file." Every time, every filing.

  1. List every authority. Every case, statute, rule and direct quote, with the sentence in the brief it supports. AI is good at pulling this list out of a draft. Let it.
  2. Open each one outside the AI tool. In Westlaw, Lexis, Fastcase or the court's own site. Confirm it exists, says what the brief says it says, and is still good law.
  3. Check every quote word for word against the source.
  4. The person who signs initials the list. If local counsel is signing, they get the list and initial it too.
  5. Keep the initialed list in the file.

It costs a fraction of one sanctions hearing. It also turns "I didn't know" into something no one at your firm can say.

The other half: what goes into the tool

Last week's post covered the other way AI gets a firm in trouble: what client information goes into the tool in the first place. A red, yellow and green list for staff, plus three questions to ask before approving any tool. If you missed it, read it here.

Where AI still earns its place

None of this is an argument for drafting without AI. First drafts, deposition summaries, chronologies and the citation list in step one are all work it does well. The rule is the same one you'd apply to a first-year associate: useful, fast, and nothing goes out the door until someone who signs has checked it.
