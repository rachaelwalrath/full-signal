What Client Information Can Go Into AI at a Five-Person Firm?

AI strategyNov 2, 20264 min read
Rachael WalrathFounder & Principal Consultant, Full Signal · LinkedIn

Someone on your team pasted a client email into a chatbot this week. You probably don't know which client, which chatbot, or what the email said. They didn't ask you first, because nobody has told them where the line is.

This post draws the line. It isn't a policy document. Those get written once, filed, and ignored by Friday. It's one page your staff can read in two minutes and tape next to the monitor.

It's already happening

In June, PagerDuty surveyed 1,250 office workers about AI at work. 88% had shared work information with public AI tools. 34% had entered customer data. 66% had used an AI tool they believed their employer didn't allow. (PagerDuty, June 2026)

Read the sample before you relax: those workers are at companies with $500 million or more in revenue. Those companies have IT departments, approved tools and written rules. A five-person firm has none of that, and I'd be surprised if the numbers were lower at yours.

The list

Green: go ahead, in any tool the firm has approved

  • Public information: published regulations, a client's public website, industry news.
  • Your own material: templates, checklists, your fee schedule, your past newsletters.
  • Examples with every identifying detail stripped out. "A client with three rental properties in two states" is green. That client's name and the property addresses are not.

Yellow: only in the firm's approved account, with the settings checked

  • Client documents: engagement letters, financial statements, contracts, correspondence.
  • Meeting notes and call transcripts.
  • Anything you'd hate to see forwarded to the wrong client.

Yellow needs three things true first. The account belongs to the firm, not to someone's personal login. The plan doesn't train on what you type. You know how long it keeps what you paste. If any of the three is unknown, yellow becomes red.

Red: never, in any tool

  • Passwords, logins and security answers.
  • Social Security numbers, tax IDs, bank and card numbers, government ID numbers.
  • Health information.
  • Anything an engagement letter or an NDA says stays with you.

The red column is short on purpose. If staff have to stop and wonder whether something is red, treat it as yellow and check the account.

Three questions before you approve any tool

  1. Does it train on what we type, and can we switch that off for the whole account, not one user at a time?
  2. How long does it keep what we paste, and can we delete it?
  3. Is the account owned by the firm, so a person's chat history doesn't leave when they do?

If the vendor answers any of these with a link to forty pages of terms and no plain yes or no, you have your answer.

What lawyers were told, and why it reaches you

Lawyers got guidance on this in 2024, because their professional rules forced the question.

The State Bar of California's ethics committee advised against putting confidential client information into any AI tool that uses inputs to train its model, and said to check a tool's security, confidentiality and data retention with an IT expert first. (Bar Association of San Francisco summary, May 2024)

The American Bar Association's Formal Opinion 512 goes further for tools that learn from what you type: get the client's informed consent first, and a boilerplate clause in the engagement letter doesn't count. (National Law Review summary, 2024)

You may not answer to a bar association. Your clients still assume that standard. A bookkeeper who loses a client's file to a chatbot doesn't get to say "I'm not a lawyer."

What the list doesn't do

It doesn't make a tool safe. It tells your staff what to keep out while you decide which tool to approve. If you haven't approved one yet, the honest answer for everything yellow is "not yet."
